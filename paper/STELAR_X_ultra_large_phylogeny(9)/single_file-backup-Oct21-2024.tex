\begin{abstract}
Summarizing individual gene trees into species phylogenies using two-step coalescent-based methods has become a standard approach in phylogenomics. 
However, gene tree estimation error (GTEE) arising from a combination of reasons (ranging from analytical factors to more biological causes, as in short gene sequences) can potentially impact the accuracy of phylogenomic inference.
We, for the first time, introduce the problem of correcting the quartet distribution induced by a set of estimated gene trees, which involves updating the weights of the quartets to better reflect their relative importance within the gene tree distribution. We present QT-WEAVER, the first method of its kind, which learns the conflicts within the quartet distribution induced by a given set of gene trees and generates an updated quartet distribution by adjusting the weights accordingly. QT-WEAVER is a general-purpose technique needing no explicit modeling of the subject system or reasons for GTEE or gene tree heterogeneity. Experimental studies on a collection of simulated and empirical data sets suggest that QT-WEAVER can effectively account for GTEE, which results in a substantial improvement in the species tree accuracy. Additionally, the concept of quartet conflicts and related algorithmic and combinatorial innovations introduced in this study will benefit various quartet-based computations. Therefore, QT-WEAVER advances the state-of-the-art in species tree estimation from gene trees in the face of GTEE. QT-WEAVER is freely available in open source form at \url{https://github.com/navidh86/QT-WEAVER}.

\section{Introduction}
High-throughput DNA sequencing is generating new genome-wide data sets for phylogenetic analyses, potentially including hundreds or even thousands of loci. The estimation of species trees from multiple genes is necessary since true gene trees can differ from each other and from the true species tree due to various processes, including gene duplication and loss, horizontal gene transfer, and incomplete lineage sorting (ILS)~\cite{M97}. ILS (also known as deep coalescence) is considered to be a dominant cause for gene tree heterogeneity, which is best understood under the coalescent model~\cite{DR06,DS05,Hud83,Nei86,Nei87,Rosenberg02,Tajima83,Takahata89}. In the presence of gene tree heterogeneity, standard methods for estimating species trees, such as concatenation (which combines sequence alignments from different loci into a single ``supermatrix'', and then computes a tree on the supermatrix) can be statistically inconsistent~\cite{roch2015likelihood,consensus-inconsistent}, and produce incorrect trees with high support~\cite{kubatko-degnan-2007}. Therefore, \textit{summary methods} that operate by combining estimated gene trees and can explicitly take gene tree discordance into account are gaining increasing attention from the systematists, and some of the coalescent based summary methods are statistically consistent~\cite{mpest,astral,larget-bioinf2010,glass,stem,svdquartet,steac,njst,astrid,stelar}. Other coalescent species-tree estimation methods include BEST~\cite{best} and *BEAST~\cite{heled-drummond}, which co-estimate gene trees and species tree from input sequence alignments.
These co-estimation methods can produce substantially more accurate trees than other methods, but are computationally intensive and do not  scale up for genome-level analyses~\cite{bayzid2013naive,smith2013target,bayzid-incomplete,leache-rannala}. 

% missing data
There has been notable progress in constructing large scale phylogenetic trees by analyzing genome-wide data, but substantial challenges remain. Assembling a complete dataset with hundreds of orthologous genes from a large set of species remains a difficult task which has downstream impact in species tree inference~\cite{hosner2016avoiding,streicher2016should,xi2016impact}. Gene trees can be incomplete due to various reasons ranging from biological processes as in gene birth and loss~\cite{mgd-amb} to biases in taxon and gene sampling, stochasticity inherent in collecting data across thousands of loci, and difficulty in sequencing and assembling the complete set of taxa of interest (see \cite{hosner2016avoiding,streicher2016should,lemmon2009effect,burleigh2009inferring} for more elaborate discussion). Incomplete gene trees may decrease the species tree accuracy~\cite{bayzid-incomplete,xi2016impact,nute2018performance} and introduce ambiguity in the tree search~\cite{sanderson2011terraces,spterrace}. Indeed, as we will show in our experimental results, incomplete gene trees substantially reduce the accuracy of the best existing coalescent based summary methods.

\add{We address the problem of missing data in phylogenomic data sets, by formulating the \textit{Quartet Distribution Imputation - \add{QDI}} problem for the first time}
, where we seek to add the missing quartets to the quartet distribution induced by a given set of incomplete gene trees. Quartet-based methods have gained substantial interest as  quartet-based phylogenetic inference is consistent in the ``Anomaly Zone''~\cite{DR06,DR09,degnan2013anomalous} and produces highly accurate species trees. ASTRAL~\cite{astral}, which is one of the most accurate and widely used coalescent-based methods, seeks to infer a species tree so that the number of induced quartets in the gene trees that are consistent with the species tree is maximized. Another approach is to infer individual quartets (with or without weights), and then amalgamate them into a single coherent species tree~\cite{strimmer_quartet_1996,tree-puzzle,ranwez2001quartet,snir_quartets_2008,qfm,wqmc,svdquartet,wqfm}. wQFM~\cite{wqfm} represents the latter category which has been shown to outperform ASTRAL.


Existing methods for adding missing taxa to the gene trees use a ``reference tree'' and attempt to add the missing species in such a way that various distance metrics (e.g., ``extra-lineage score'', Robinson-Foulds distance) are optimized with respect to the given reference tree~\cite{bayzid-incomplete,octal}. However, obtaining a reasonably accurate reference tree in the presence of missing data is difficult~\cite{octal}, which subsequently affects the tree completion steps as the reference tree can be far from the true tree. In this study, we present QT-GILD (\textbf{Q}uar\textbf{t}et based \textbf{G}ene tree \textbf{I}mputation using \textbf{D}eep \textbf{L}earning) -- a novel deep learning based technique which learns the quartet distribution induced by the given set of incomplete gene trees using an especially tailored autoencoder model, and subsequently generates a complete set of quartets. In doing so, it not only imputes the missing quartets, but also attempts \add{to correct} the quartets present in the given set of estimated and incomplete gene trees by leveraging the underlying quartet distribution. Therefore, the complete set of quartets generated by QT-GILD is often closer to the set of quartets present in the true gene trees than to those present in the estimated gene trees. Thus, QT-GILD obviates the need for a reference tree as well as accounts for gene tree estimation error. Our experimental results, on a collection of simulated and real biological data sets covering a wide range of model conditions, indeed show that amalgamating the imputed set of quartets generated by QT-GILD can remarkably improve the accuracy of species tree inference. Additionally, we particularly aimed for making QT-GILD an unsupervised machine learning technique. Unsupervised learning approaches have advantages over supervised methods particularly when the data are heterogeneous, which are often so with various phylogenomic \add{datasets} and therefore the supervised models trained on a particular set of incomplete gene trees may not be generalizable to a new set of input gene trees. Furthermore, QT-GILD is a general-purpose approach which  requires no explicit modeling of the reasons of gene tree heterogeneity or missing data, making it less vulnerable to model mis-specification.
QT-GILD is the first of its kind which advances the state-of-the-art in species tree estimation in the presence of missing data.

\section{Quartet Distribution Correction Problem}
\subsection{Problem Definition}
Let $\mathcal{G} =  \{g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_k\}$ be a set of $k$ gene trees, where each $g_i$ is a tree on taxon set $S_i \subseteq S$ (i.e., any gene tree $g_i$ can be on the full set $S$ of $n$ taxa or can be on a subset $S_i$ of taxa, making the gene tree incomplete). For a set of four taxa $a,b,c,d \in S$, the quartet tree $ab|cd$ denotes the unrooted quartet tree in which the pair $a, b$ is separated from the pair $c, d$ by an edge. Note that there are three alternative quartet topologies ($ab|cd, ac|bd, ad|bc$) for four taxa.
%Note that on every set of four species, there are three possible unrooted quartet trees (simply called ``quartets''). Also note that any gene tree $g_i$ on the set $S_i$ of taxa can induce a single quartet tree on $a, b, c, d$ (given $a,b,c,d \in S_i$). 
Let $\mathcal{Q}_i$ be the set of quartets in $g_i$. Therefore, $\mathcal{Q} = \mathcal{Q}_1 \cup \mathcal{Q}_2 \cup \ldots \cup \mathcal{Q}_k$ is the multi-set of quartets present in $\mathcal{G}$. Note that there are $n_i \choose 4$ quartets in $\mathcal{Q}_i$, and $3$$n \choose 4$ quartets in $\mathcal{Q}$ are unique, as there are three alternative quartet topologies for a set of four taxa. Let $\mathcal{Q}_u$ be the set of these $3$$n \choose 4$ quartets and $w_i$ ($0 \le w_i \le k$) be the frequency of a quartet $q_i \in \mathcal{Q}_u$ in $\mathcal{Q}$ (i.e., the number of times  a quartet $q_i$ appears in the input gene trees). Then the gene tree frequency (GTF) based weighted quartet distribution $\mathcal{QD}$ of $\mathcal{G}$ contains all possible quartets on $n$ taxa along with their frequencies in the gene trees. That means, this is defined as the set \{$(q_1,w_1), (q_2,w_2), \ldots, (q_{3n \choose 4},w_{3n \choose 4})\}$ of $3$$n \choose 4$ tuples. Weighted quartet amalgamation techniques such as wQFM and wQMC take such distributions as input.

Let $\mathcal{G_T}$ and $\mathcal{G_E}$ be the set of true and estimated gene trees, respectively. Consequently, the definitions of $\mathcal{Q_T}$, $\mathcal{Q_E}$, $\mathcal{QD_T}$, $\mathcal{QD_E}$ extend in an obvious way. Note that, in the absence of any gene tree estimation error, the weight $w_i$ of $q_i$ in both $\mathcal{QD_T}$ and $\mathcal{QD_E}$ will be identical. We now define the quartet distribution correction (QDC) problem as follows.

\subsection{Proposed Methodology: Identifying Quartet Conflicts}
\label{sec:qt-conflict}


Gene tree estimation errors result in inaccuracies in the induced distribution of quartets, resulting in incorrect relative importance of the quartets. %For example, GTEE can infer incorrect ``dominant'' quartets. For a set of four taxa $a, b, c, d$, the dominant quartet (out of three possible quartets $ab|cd$, $ac|bd$, and $bc|ad$) is defined to be the quartet with the highest weight (i.e., the most frequent quartet topology). A dominant quartet topology is the statistically consistent estimate for the true evolutionary history among a group of four taxa since there are no \textit{anomalous} 4-leaf unrooted gene trees~\cite{DR09,degnan2013anomalous}. 
The divergence from the true quartet distribution (e.g., incorrectly inferred dominant quartets due to GTEE) negatively impacts the accuracy of species tree estimation. To address this, our objective is to adjust the weights of the quartets in such a way that the estimated quartet distribution better reflects the true underlying distribution. Importantly, we aim to achieve this without relying on a reference species tree or additional data sources, such as gene sequence alignments. Therefore, we propose to learn the inherent patterns of consistency within the input quartets and update their weights accordingly. Our approach is based on detecting patterns of ``conflicts'' among quartets, where a set of quartets is considered \textit{conflicting} if they cannot be simultaneously satisfied by a single tree. Therefore, we try to learn the inherent patterns of conflicts within the quartets induced by the estimated gene trees and update the weights accordingly. Note that there are three alternative topologies for a set $\{a,b,c,d\}$ of four taxa: $ab|cd, ac|bd$, and $ad|bc$. We call these quartets ``topological variants'' of each other, and clearly they are conflicting. We now formalize the concept of conflicts between quartets in Theorem~\ref{thm:conflict}, which generalizes how the number of common taxa between two quartets affects the number of trees that can simultaneously satisfy both quartets.


The leaves in a tree $T$ is denoted by $L(T)$.
Every edge $e$ in an unrooted leaf-labeled tree $T$ defines a bipartition (or, split) $\pi(e)$ on the leaves $L(T)$ (induced by the deletion of $e$). 


\begin{theorem} 
\label{thm:conflict}
Let $q_1$ and $q_2$ be two quartets drawn from a set of taxa. The number of binary trees on $L(q_1) \cup L(q_2)$ that simultaneously satisfy both $q_1$ and $q_2$ is determined by the number of taxa shared between them. Specifically, let $ct$ denote the number of taxa common to both quartets. Then the possible values of $ct$ are 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4, and the number of binary trees that satisfy both $q_1$ and $q_2$ is as follows: 


\textbf{Case 1: $ct = 0, 1, 2$} \ When the number of common taxa between $q_1$ and $q_2$ is less than 3, i.e., $ct = 0, 1, 2$, there will always be multiple binary trees on $L(q_1) \cup L(q_2)$ that satisfy both quartets.

\textbf{Case 2: $ct = 3$} \ When $q_1$ and $q_2$ share exactly three common taxa ($ct = 3$), meaning each quartet has one unique taxon relative to the other, two sub-cases arise: 
    \begin{enumerate} 
        \item If the unique taxon in $q_1$ replaces the unique taxon in $q_2$ (meaning that when the unique taxon in quartet $q_1$ is replaced by the unique taxon in the other quartet $q_2$, the resulting topology will match that of $q_2$), there are exactly three distinct binary trees on $L(q_1) \cup L(q_2)$ that satisfy both quartets $q_1$ and $q_2$.
        \item In all other configurations where the quartets share 3 common taxa, there is exactly one binary tree on $L(q_1) \cup L(q_2)$ that satisfies both quartets. 
    \end{enumerate}

\textbf{Case 3: $ct = 4$} \ When $q_1$ and $q_2$ share all 4 taxa ($ct = 4$), no binary tree can satisfy both quartets. %, as they correspond to topologically distinct variants of each other.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Algorithm: QT-WEAVER}
\label{sec:qt-weaver-algorithm}

Let $\mathcal{QD_E} = \{ (q_1,w_1), (q_2,w_2), \ldots, (q_{3n \choose 4},w_{3n \choose 4}) \}$ be the set of weighted quartets of a set $\mathcal{G_E}$ of estimated gene trees, $S$ be the set of all taxa present in  $\mathcal{G_E}$ and $\mathcal{Q} = \{ q_1, q_2, \ldots,q_{3n \choose 4} \} $ be the set of all quartets in $\mathcal{QD_E}$. The algorithm takes the set $\mathcal{QD_E}$ as input and for each quartet $q_i \in \mathcal{QD_E}$, the algorithm adjusts the weight $w_i$ to $w_i'$ using the concept of quartet conflict we introduced in Section~\ref{sec:qt-conflict}. We maintain a set \( \mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{Q} \), which represents the set of quartets whose weights have already been adjusted.  Initially, \( \mathcal{A} = \emptyset \), indicating that no quartet weights have been adjusted at the start of the algorithm.

We iterate over the set of quartets, $q \in \mathcal{Q}$. If a quartet $q$ is already adjusted, i.e., $ q \in \mathcal{A} $, we skip the quartet. Otherwise, let $ \mathcal{T} = \{q_1, q_2, q_3\}$ be the set of three possible quartets on $L(q)$. %Without loss of generality, if we assume the quartet,  $q = ab|cd$ is our current quartet, then $ \mathcal{T} = \{ ab|cd, ac|bd, ad|bc \}$. 
We then adjust the weights of the quartets in $\mathcal{T}$ and insert them to $\mathcal{A}$, i.e., $\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \mathcal{A} \cup \mathcal{T}$. To adjust the weight for each quartet $q_i$ in $\mathcal{T}$, we construct $Z_i$, a set of all quartet pairs conflicting with $q_i$. %\addb{it is not clear. What is the leafset of Z?}

$$
Z_i = \{(q_j, q_k) \mid q_j, q_k \in \mathcal{Q} \text{ and } q_j, q_k \text{ are in conflict with } q_i\}
$$

We do this by iterating over the set of taxa not present in $q_i$, i.e. $S \setminus L(q_i)$. 
For each $t \in S \setminus L(q_i)$, we obtain a set of 28 quartet pairs in conflict with the quartet $q_i$ (Corollary \ref{cor:unique-conflict}). We add all such pairs to $Z_i$. Thus, $Z_i$ contains $28\times|S \setminus L(q_i)| = 28 \times (n-4)$ pairs of quartets. Next, we compute the \textit{conflict score} for each of the quartets in $\mathcal{T}$ using the PoW or MoW based formula in Equation \eqref{eq:conflict_score} or Equation \ref{eq:min_conflict_score} (this is a user defined configuration in our algorithm).

Note that the conflict score of a quartet $q_i$ indicates a level of inconsistency of $q_i$ with respect to other quartets in the distribution. Therefore, a quartet with a higher conflict score should have a lower readjusted weight (i.e., we want to decrease its relative importance as this quartet has a higher degree of conflict with others). Therefore, we obtain $\Tilde{w_i}$ by dividing $ w_i $, the original weight of the quartet $ q_i $, by its conflict score $C(q_i)$.

$$
\Tilde{w_i} = \frac{w_i}{C(q_i)}
$$

%Here, $ \tilde{w}_i $ represents the temporarily adjusted weight of the quartet $ q_i $. 
This operation on three quartets in $\mathcal{T}$ denormalizes the weights of the quartets, which must be restored to their original sum $s = w_1+w_2+w_3$. % When the gene trees are complete (without any missing taxa), $s$ equals  $k$ (the number of gene trees in input $\mathcal{G}$). Otherwise, $s \leq k$. %Let $q_x, q_y, q_z$ be the three topological variants of a quartet with adjusted weights $\Tilde{w_x}, \Tilde{w_y}, \Tilde{w_z}$ respectively. 
To normalize these weights, we multiply each by $\frac{s}{\Tilde{w_1} + \Tilde{w_1} + \Tilde{w_3}}$ so that their sum equals their original sum $s$. The final normalized weight $w_x'$ is given by:
$$
w_x' = \frac{ s }{\Tilde{w_a} + \Tilde{w_2} + \Tilde{w_3}} \cdot \Tilde{w_x}
$$

\subsection{QT-WEAVER configurations}

As discussed in Sections \ref{sec:qt-conflict}, \ref{sec:qt-weaver-algorithm}, we can define the conflict score of a quartet $q$ using a product of weights (PoW) based approach (Equation.~\ref{eq:conflict_score}) or a minimum of weights (MoW) based approach (Equation~\ref{eq:min_conflict_score}). %both the regular" and "minimum" weighting schemes of QT-WEAVER for updating quartet weights. 
Additionally, beyond using all 28 conflicting sets that a quartet $q$ belongs to for each taxon $e \notin L(q)$, we explore subsets of these conflicting sets, analyzing subsets of sizes four, six, and eight in this study. Thus, we have explored eight configurations (two weighting schemes combined with four conflicting set subsets). We empirical exploration using the 15-taxon datasets (results presented in Appendix \ref{app:configurations}) suggests that MoW-based approach is preferable over the PoW-based scheme. Moreover, utilizing six conflicting sets is both computationally faster and yields better performance across all conditions. Therefore, for the remaining experiments, we run QT-WEAVER withe configuration that uses 6 (out of 28) conflicting sets and the MoW-based approach to update quartet weights.

\subsection{Measurements}

We evaluated the accuracy of the estimated trees on simulated datasets by comparing them to the model species tree using the normalized Robinson-Foulds (RF) distance~\cite{robinson_comparison_1981}, which measures tree error based on the sum of bipartitions (splits) present in one tree but absent in the other. Additionally, we assessed quartet scores, reflecting the number of quartets from the gene trees that are consistent with the estimated species tree. As all estimated trees in this study are binary, the False Positive (FP), False Negative (FN), and RF rates are identical. For the biological dataset, we compared the inferred species trees with those reported in the scientific literature. Multiple replicates under different model conditions were analyzed, and statistical significance between methods was determined using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test (with $\alpha = 0.05$). To assess the accuracy of quartet distributions, we compared both corrected and uncorrected distributions to the true quartet distributions derived from the true gene trees.

\subsection{Results on 15-taxon dataset}

In the simulated 15-taxon datasets, we evaluated the performance on varying gene tree estimation error using 100bp and 1000bp sequence lengths and on varying numbers of gene trees (100 and 1000). Similar to 37-taxon dataset, wQFM+QT-WEAVER consistently outperforms ASTRAL and wQFM across all four model conditions (Figure \ref{fig:rf_15_taxon_methods}). In particular, wQFM+QT-WEAVER is significantly better ($p<<0.05$) than ASTRAL on challenging model conditions with short sequences (100bp), i.e., high amounts of gene tree estimation error. As expected, all methods showed lower error rates with increasing sequence lengths and increasing numbers of genes. 

Similar to 37-taxon dataset, we investigated the divergence of original (uncorrected) and corrected (by QT-WEAVER) quartet distributions from true quartet distributions (see Figure~\ref{fig:rf_15_taxon_methods}(c)-(d)) and the quartet scores of different estimated species trees with respect to both estimated and true gene trees (see Appendix~\ref{app:37-taxon}) and observed similar trends. 
The Jensen-Shannon divergence clearly improves after correction when the gene tree estimation error is high (100bp). On the other hand, when the distribution has low amounts of GTEE with relatively long (1000bp) sequences and thus very low divergence to begin with (less than 5 percent), correcting it does not seem to reduce the difference. Thus, the quartet distributions inferred from gene trees with high estimation error realign with the true distribution after correction using QT-WEAVER. Interestingly, even though the divergence increases slightly for the more accurate distributions (1000bp), the corrected distribution as a whole seems to be more representative of the true distribution, as supported by the better RF rates.

We again assess the quartet scores like we did for the 37-taxon dataset in Tables \ref{tab:15-quartet-scores-estimated} and \ref{tab:15-quartet-scores-true}, and observed similar trends. wQFM+QT-WEAVER achieves higher and closer (to true quartet score) quartet scores than ASTRAL and wQFM across all four model conditions when the quartets scores are computed based on true gene trees. These results support our claim that QT-WEAVER effectively accounts for gene tree estimation error while imputing the missing quartets.

\section{Results on biological dataset}

\paragraph{Avian dataset.}
We now reanalyze the avian biological dataset (\cite{jarvis2014}), which has 14,446 genes across 48 taxa. The genes include exons, introns, and UCEs. This dataset contains high levels of gene tree discordance likely driven by rapid radiation events in the evolutionary history of these species. We extend the analysis presented by Mahbub et al. \cite{wqfm} where they compared ASTRAL, wQFM, and wQMC with the reference tree (Binned MP-EST). We include wQFM-corrected in this comparison (Fig. \ref{fig:avian}). We used the ``regular" weighting scheme for correction in this scenario as it generated a better tree than its counterpart.

\begin{figure}[t!]         
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures_new/avian-combined-v1.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Analyses of the avian dataset using ASTRAL and wQFM (before and after correction).} 
    Branch supports are computed based on quartet-based local posterior probability~\cite{sayyari2016fast} (multiplied by 100). All BS values are 100\% except where noted.}
    \label{fig:avian}
\end{figure}

All three estimated trees are highly congruent with the reference binned tree, with wQFM-corrected being the most congruent and ASTRAL the least. Still, all three failed to reconstruct some well-established sub-groups by the
avian phylogenomics project and other studies.

The wQFM trees are almost identical, with two noticeable differences. The first is the placement of the Caprimulgimorphae clade. Secondly, and more importantly, wQFM-corrected was able to correctly resolve the Cursores clade (crane and killdeer), which both wQFM and ASTRAL failed to do.

All three methods successfully reconstructed the well-established Australaves clade (passeriformes, parrots, falcons, and
seriemas). They also recovered the Afroaves, Core Waterbird, and Caprimulgimorphae clades successfully.
ASTRAL failed to recover Otidimorphae, unlike both the wQFM methods.  All three failed to recover Columbea but were able to recover the constituent clades Columbimorphae (mesite, sandgrouse, and pigeon) and Phoenicopterimorphae (flamingo and grebe).



\subsection{Running Time}
The running time of QT-WEAVER solely depends on the number of taxa $n$, which determines the number of unique quartets ($3$$n \choose 4$) in the weighted quartet distribution.
All analyses were run on the same machine with AMD Ryzen 7 5800H CPU (8 cores), 16GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU (6GB memory). The simulated 15-taxon and 37-taxon datasets took 1.65 and 242 seconds on average per replicate. The biological avian dataset with 48 taxa took 983 seconds to run. As the number of quartets grows exponentially with the number of taxa, so does the runtime.

\section{Conclusions}

This study, for the first time, introduces the quartet imputation problem and shows the power and feasibility of applying deep learning techniques for imputing quartet distributions. %OUr proposed QT-GILD, with its deep autoencoder model with  self-supervision (using masking), effectively learns the underlying quartet distributions and account for gene tree estimation error.
Our proposed QT-GILD not only imputes the missing quartets but it can also ``correct’’ the estimated quartets -- resulting in a set of quartets that is often closer to the quartet distribution of true gene trees than that of the estimated gene trees, and thereby accounting for gene tree estimation error. Experimental studies using both simulated and real biological dataset suggest that QT-GILD may result in dramatic improvements in species tree accuracy. 
Therefore, the idea of estimating species trees by imputing  quartet distributions has merit and should be pursued and used in future phylogenomic studies. %However, this study can be extended in several directions, especially 
As an immediate extension of the current study we plan to evaluate QT-GILD on a diverse set of real biological datasets as the  pattern of missing data is sufficiently complex and heterogeneous across various datasets. Automatic selection of various hyper parameters of this architecture is another important research avenue.
Because QT-GILD tries to learn the overall quartet distribution guided by a self-supervised feedback loop and correct for gene tree estimation error, investigating its application beyond imputing incomplete gene trees where the goal would be to improve estimated gene tree distributions is another interesting direction to take. In this regard, we can mask a reasonably large number of quartets in the estimated complete quartet distribution and then impute them with QT-GILD, utilizing its error correcting utility. QT-GILD is currently not scalable to large datasets with hundreds of taxa, therefore this study was limited to small to moderate size datasets. Future study needs to develop a scalable variant of QT-GILD and assess its performance on large dataset.
